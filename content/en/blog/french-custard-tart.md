The French flan pâtissier nearly always fails the same way: it wobbles too much, weeps water after an hour, or cracks as it cools. All three share one cause, the proportion of starch, and only one is fixed in the oven.

## What holds a flan together

A flan pâtissier is not an egg-set custard like a crème caramel. It is a **pastry cream baked in the oven**, and it is the starch that structures it, not the eggs.

Starch swells and gels at around 85 °C. In gelling, it traps the water from the milk: that is what lets a flan slice cleanly instead of slumping.

Too little starch and it wobbles and releases water as it cools. Too much and it turns rubbery.

| Starch per litre of milk | Result |
|---|---|
| 60 g | Too loose, weeps |
| **90 to 100 g** | **Slices cleanly, stays creamy** |
| 130 g and above | Firm, rubbery, tastes of flour |

I work at **90 g of cornflour per litre**, with 4 whole eggs. Cornflour gives a silkier texture than wheat flour, which leaves a perceptible taste.

## The custard

For a 22 cm deep tin:

- 1 litre whole milk
- 90 g cornflour
- 200 g sugar
- 4 eggs
- 1 vanilla pod, or 2 tsp extract

**Whole milk is not negotiable.** With semi-skimmed the flan is paler, less fragrant, and weeps more: the fat takes part in holding the water.

Infuse the vanilla in the hot milk **off the heat, covered, for 20 minutes**. A pod thrown into boiling milk gives far less flavour.

## Pre-cooking the custard, the step that changes everything

Many recipes pour the raw custard onto the pastry and let the oven do the work. That is the main cause of a weeping flan.

The right method: **cook the custard like a pastry cream**, on the hob, until it thickens firmly and bubbles lazily for a full minute. The starch is then already gelled and the oven only has to colour it and finish setting it.

That minute of boiling counts: below it the starch has not finished its job and the flan will stay liquid in the centre however long it bakes.

## The pastry, and the soggy base question

Shortcrust or [sweet shortcrust](/en/blog/why-shortcrust-shrinks/) both work, provided they are **blind baked** before receiving the custard. A raw base under a litre of cream has no chance.

Fifteen minutes at 180 °C with a weight, then five without, exactly as for a tart. The base must be dry to the touch and lightly golden.

A flan without pastry exists and holds perfectly well: the custard is structured enough to stand alone. Butter and line the tin with baking paper.

## Baking

**180 °C for 35 to 45 minutes**, until the surface is **frankly patched with brown**, almost scorched in places. That is the signature of a bakery flan, and it is where most people stop too early out of caution.

What must not happen is the custard **boiling** in the oven: large bubbles at the surface mean the cream is splitting and will weep. If it boils, drop to 165 °C and extend the time.

**The flan must cool completely before slicing**, at least 4 hours and ideally overnight in the fridge. Sliced warm, it always slumps.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does the surface crack?**
Too fierce a bake, or too abrupt a cooling. Leaving the flan in the switched-off oven with the door ajar for 15 minutes limits the thermal shock.

**Can I use cream instead of milk?**
Replacing 200 ml of the milk with cream gives a richer, denser flan. Beyond that the texture turns heavy and loses the wobble that makes it interesting.

**Why does mine taste of flour?**
Either wheat flour was used instead of cornflour, or the custard did not boil enough. A full minute of boiling is enough to remove that taste.

**How long does it keep?**
Three days in the fridge, covered. It does not freeze: the starch releases its water on thawing and the texture is lost.
