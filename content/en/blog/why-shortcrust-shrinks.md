You roll the pastry out, line the tin neatly, and the sides stand up straight. Twenty minutes later the sides have slid two centimetres down and the tart has no rim left.

This is not a recipe problem. It is almost always a gluten and timing problem.

## What happens in the tin

Even shortcrust, which contains little water, develops [gluten](/en/blog/gluten-free-cookies/) as soon as you work it. That network is **elastic**: stretched, it tries to return to where it started, exactly like a rubber band being released.

When you roll the pastry, you stretch it. If you put it straight into the oven, the network is still under tension. The heat softens the butter, the pastry loses its rigidity, and the tension wins: the pastry contracts towards the centre.

Resting is therefore not about "letting it cool". It is about **letting the network relax**. That is the difference between a stretched rubber band and a slack one.

## The four causes, in order of frequency

**Too short a rest.** Thirty minutes is not enough. You need **two hours in the fridge for the ball of pastry, then another thirty minutes once the tin is lined.** That second rest, after lining, is the one almost everyone skips, and it is the most effective: the pastry is under most tension right after lining.

**Overworked pastry.** Shortcrust is mixed until it just comes together, not a second longer. If it turns smooth and supple like bread dough, the gluten is already too developed. It should stay slightly uneven.

**Too much water.** Water is what lets gluten form. Shortcrust needs very little, and often none at all if the egg is enough. Every spoonful added to "rescue" a dry dough increases the shrinkage.

**Pastry stretched to fit the tin.** If the disc is too small and you pull it to reach the edges, it will return to exactly its original size in the oven. Roll it wider than you need and let it drop into the tin without forcing.

## The method that fixes it

**Line without stretching.** Roll the pastry onto the pin, unroll it over the tin, then push it down into the corners with your fingertips, never by pulling.

**Prick the base with a fork**, every couple of centimetres. That prevents blistering rather than shrinking, but you may as well do it at the same time.

**Thirty minutes in the freezer before baking.** This is more effective than the fridge and replaces the second rest if you are in a hurry. Pastry that goes into the oven frozen keeps its sides because the butter stays solid long enough for the structure to set.

**Blind bake with a real weight.** Baking paper and dried beans, right up to the top of the sides. A weight sitting only in the base does not hold the walls. I always bank the beans up against the sides, that is where they do the work. With no oven at all, there is always the [no-bake crisp base](/en/blog/no-bake-chocolate-cake/) route.

## The blind baking schedule

| Stage | Temperature | Time |
|---|---|---|
| With the weight | 180 °C | 15 min |
| Without the weight, to dry | 180 °C | 5 to 8 min |
| With the filling, if it bakes | 170 to 180 °C | Per the recipe |

The weightless stage is not optional. It is what dries the base and stops it going soggy once filled. The pastry should be **dry to the touch and lightly golden**, not merely pale.

## The rescue, when it has already happened

Shrunken pastry does not come back. However, if you spot it halfway through baking, you can take the tin out, gently push the sides back up with the back of a spoon while the pastry is still soft, replace the weight, and finish baking. The result is imperfect but the tart is still fillable.

If the sides have collapsed completely, there is the rimless option: fill a flat sheet of pastry, fold the edges roughly over the filling, and embrace the rustic tart. That is what I do when the children are waiting, in the same spirit as a [cake without an oven](/en/blog/cake-without-oven/) on the evening of a breakdown.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does freezer resting really replace fridge resting?**
It replaces the second rest, not the first. Gluten relaxation takes time, not just cold, and it barely happens when the pastry is frozen. The ideal remains two hours in the fridge then thirty minutes in the freezer.

**Does sweet pastry shrink as much?**
Less, because it contains more sugar and butter, and therefore proportionally less hydrated flour. But it still shrinks if it has not rested.

**Can shortcrust be pressed straight into the tin?**
Yes, by pushing it out with your fingertips without a rolling pin. That is in fact the safest method against shrinkage, since nothing gets stretched. A slightly less even base is the only drawback.

**Why does mine shrink even after resting overnight?**
If the rest is long and the problem persists, the culprit is the mixing: the pastry was worked until smooth. Even a long rest does not fully undo a heavily developed gluten network.
