A whipped ganache is a classic ganache to which cold cream has been added, which has then rested and been whipped. All three steps matter, but **the rest decides the outcome**, and it is the one people skip.

## Why the rest is compulsory

A freshly made ganache is a warm emulsion: the fats from the chocolate and the cream are dispersed as liquid droplets. Whipped in that state it does not aerate, it merely thickens a little and then splits.

In the cold, those droplets **partly crystallise**. That crystalline structure is what traps air when you whip, exactly as in whipped cream. Without it, there is nothing to stabilise the bubbles.

The minimum is **6 hours in the fridge**, and overnight is markedly better. This is not a comfort precaution: at 3 hours I get a glossy cream that collapses within twenty minutes.

## Ratios, by type of chocolate

The rule: one part hot cream to make the ganache, then **one and a half to two parts cold cream** added afterwards.

| Chocolate | Hot cream | Cold cream | Resulting set |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dark 55 to 60 % | 100 g per 100 g | 200 g | Firm, pipes cleanly |
| Milk | 100 g per 150 g | 250 g | Softer, sweeter |
| White | 100 g per 200 g | 250 g | The most fragile, splits fast |

White chocolate needs twice the solids of dark for the same hold, because it contains no cocoa solids: only cocoa butter and sugar give it structure.

**All the cream must be full fat, at least 30 %.** Low-fat cream will not whip, however long it rests. It is the most frequent mistake and the only one that can never be rescued.

## The method

**Make the ganache.** Heat the first cream to a simmer, pour it over the chopped chocolate in three goes, stirring in small circles from the centre. The mixture must turn smooth and glossy before the next addition.

**Add the cold cream.** All at once, into the still-warm ganache, and stir without whipping. The mixture turns liquid, which is normal.

**Cover with cling film touching the surface and chill.** Overnight.

**Whip, but not too far.** Medium speed, not maximum. The ganache goes from liquid to soft in two or three minutes. **Stop as soon as it holds on the whisk**, not a second later.

## The point of no return

An over-whipped ganache turns grainy, then separates into a greasy mass and a clear liquid. That is the cocoa butter clumping, and no amount of whisking will fix it.

The only rescue: **melt the whole thing over a bain-marie, add 20 ml of cold cream, stir, and rest it again overnight.** You start over, but you lose nothing.

The warning sign is visible: the ganache loses its shine and turns matte. That is the moment to stop.

## What it is for, and what it is not for

It is made to be **piped**: filling macarons, building a layer cake, filling choux buns. It holds for hours at room temperature without slumping, which whipped cream does not.

It is not made for **coating**. To cover a cake with a smooth glossy layer you want the classic ganache, unwhipped, poured warm, like the one mentioned in the article on [icing without icing sugar](/en/blog/icing-without-icing-sugar/).

It is also wrong for a [no-bake cake](/en/blog/no-bake-chocolate-cake/), which needs a dense mass to hold itself up, not an aerated cream.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can it be flavoured?**
Yes, by infusing the hot cream with whatever you like (vanilla, zest, tea, spices) then straining it before pouring over the chocolate. Top up the liquid lost in the infusion or the ratios shift.

**How long does it keep?**
Unwhipped, 3 days in the fridge. Whipped, use it within 24 hours: it keeps firming up and turns brittle.

**Can it be whipped in a stand mixer?**
Yes, and it is safer provided you stay at medium speed and watch it. A mixer at full speed crosses the point of no return in seconds.

**Why is my ganache grainy from the start?**
The chocolate was probably overheated when making the base ganache, beyond roughly 50 °C. The fault is there before the rest; whipping only reveals it.
