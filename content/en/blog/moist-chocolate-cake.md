Moistness is not a quality you add, it is a structure you avoid over-building. A dry cake is one where the flour and the eggs did their job all the way to the end.

## The three settings that decide

**How much flour.** Flour gives structure. The less there is, the softer the cake, and the harder it is to turn out. Below 30 g per 200 g of chocolate you tip over into a flourless fondant, which is a different cake.

**How long it bakes.** A chocolate cake carries on cooking in its tin after it leaves the oven. Baking to the "perfectly clean skewer" stage always gives a dry cake once cool.

**The temperature.** At 180 °C the edges set fast while the centre lags. At 160 °C the bake is more even and leaves a far wider margin for error.

## The ratios

For a 22 cm tin:

- 200 g dark chocolate, 55 %
- 150 g butter
- 150 g sugar
- 4 eggs
- **50 g flour**
- a pinch of salt

Fifty grams of flour is little, and deliberately so. Most recipes use 100 to 120: the cake holds together better, travels better, and is noticeably less moist.

**Separating the eggs changes a lot.** Whipping the whites and folding them in last gives a lighter crumb without touching the flour. It is the one extra technique genuinely worth the trouble here.

## Baking, in detail

**160 °C, 22 to 25 minutes** for this tin.

The cues, in the order you check them:

- The cake has **risen and is just starting to come back down** at the edges.
- The centre **still wobbles slightly** when you move the tin.
- A skewer 3 cm from the edge comes out **almost** clean; in the centre it comes out damp.

That last point surprises people. A clean skewer in the centre means you are five minutes past.

In my oven, which runs too hot underneath, I drop to 155 °C and add 3 minutes, with an empty tray on the shelf below. Without it the base is done ten minutes before the rest. It is the same reflex as for a [cake without an oven](/en/blog/cake-without-oven/): compensate for badly spread heat rather than suffer it.

## Cooling, which matters just as much

**Leave the cake in its tin for 15 minutes**, then turn it out onto a rack. Turned out scalding, it breaks; left in the tin until cold, it goes damp from condensation.

A chocolate cake is better **the next day**. Wrapped once cool, it redistributes its moisture and the crumb evens out. That is true here and false for almost everything else in baking.

## The mistakes that dry it out

**Overheated chocolate.** Beyond roughly 50 °C it turns grainy and releases its fat. The cake is then greasy on top and dry inside.

**Too much sugar.** Past 200 g for these quantities, sugar draws water and dries the crumb, which is counter-intuitive.

**Opening the oven too early.** Before 15 minutes, the drop in temperature sinks the centre, and it never comes back.

**A tin that is too big.** In a 26 cm tin the same batter is 2 cm deep and bakes in 15 minutes. The tin is part of the recipe.

## Frequently asked questions

**How does this differ from a fondant or a shop-bought moelleux?**
A fondant has even less flour, sometimes none, and is eaten warm. A moelleux keeps a crumb. Industrial versions add emulsifiers that hold water for weeks, which no home recipe does.

**Can it be made without butter?**
Yes, with 120 g of neutral oil, applying the same logic as for [brownies without butter](/en/blog/brownie-without-butter/). The crumb stays moist, the top crust disappears.

**How long does it keep?**
Three days wrapped at room temperature, and it is better on the second. In the fridge it hardens: that is the butter crystallising, so take it out an hour before serving.

**How do I know my oven is accurate?**
An oven thermometer costs about eight euros and settles the question once. Most domestic ovens are 20 to 30 °C off, and that gap explains half of all dry cakes.
