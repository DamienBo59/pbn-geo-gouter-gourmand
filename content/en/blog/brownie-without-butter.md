Taking butter out of a brownie is not taking out fat: it is taking out **three things at once**, and only the first is obvious.

## What butter does in a brownie

It provides **fat**, which coats the flour and stops gluten developing. That is what gives the dense, fudgy texture, the opposite of an airy cake.

It provides **water**, about 16 % of its weight. That water evaporates during baking and helps lift the surface.

And it provides **milk proteins**, responsible for the flavour and above all for the thin crackled top, the thing that separates a good brownie from a good chocolate cake.

A substitute that only replaces the first of the three gives a decent but flat brownie, with no crust and no depth. That is the most common outcome.

## The three substitutes that work

| Substitute | Amount per 100 g butter | What you get | What you lose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neutral oil | 80 g | The fudgiest of the three, holds well | The crackled top, and some flavour |
| Nut butter | 110 g | Lots of flavour, tight texture | Softness, it turns compact |
| Apple sauce | 100 g | A light, almost damp brownie | The fudginess, and it dries in 48 h |

**Oil is the best default.** Use 20 % less than the butter, because oil is 100 % fat against 82 % for butter. Matching the weight gives an oily brownie that leaves a mark on the plate.

**Almond or peanut butter** works very well, but it brings its own flavour and dry matter. Add 20 ml of milk to compensate.

**Apple sauce** changes the result most. I keep it for brownies eaten the same day: by the next morning they are dry.

## Getting the crackled top back

This is the part most butter-free recipes give up on, although it can be recovered.

The crust comes from **dissolved sugar rising to the surface** and crystallising. It depends far less on butter than on how you treat the eggs and sugar.

The move that brings it back: **whisk the eggs and sugar for a good three minutes**, until the mixture pales and nearly doubles, before folding in the melted chocolate. It is that foam which bakes into the thin glossy film.

Two further conditions: the melted chocolate must be **warm, not hot**, or it collapses the foam; and the batter must not wait, it goes straight into the oven.

## The corrected ratios

For a 20 x 20 cm tin, my reference base:

- 200 g dark chocolate, 55 %
- **80 g neutral oil** (instead of 100 g butter)
- 150 g sugar
- 3 eggs
- 60 g flour
- a pinch of salt

The salt is not a detail. Salted butter brings roughly 0.8 g of salt per 100 g; without it, an oil-based brownie tastes flat. A good pinch puts it right.

## Baking changes too

Without butter the batter heats slightly faster. I drop from 180 to **170 °C**, for 22 to 25 minutes.

The cue is the same as for a classic brownie and it is counter-intuitive: **the centre should still wobble** when you take the tin out. A brownie baked through in the oven will be dry once cool. As with [gluten-free cookies](/en/blog/gluten-free-cookies/), the window is narrow and it is better to be early than late.

In my oven, which runs far too hot underneath, I put the tin on the upper shelf with an empty tray below. Without it, the base sets hard long before the centre.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can margarine replace butter?**
Yes, at the same weight, and it is the most faithful substitute for texture since it also contains water. It brings no milk proteins, so the crust stays fainter.

**Does olive oil work?**
A mild olive oil, yes, and it pairs well with 70 % chocolate. A fruity or peppery oil dominates the chocolate, which is not necessarily a fault but makes a different cake.

**Why is my butter-free brownie crumbly?**
Too much flour, or not enough fat after the conversion. Check you used 80 g of oil by weight and not 80 % of the volume, which is not the same thing.

**Does it keep as long?**
With oil, yes, four days in an airtight tin, and even slightly better than with butter because oil does not crystallise when cold. With apple sauce, two days at most.
